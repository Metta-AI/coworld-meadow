The meadow test: what kills a commons run by language models

We seated eight Claude instances at a shared, regenerating resource — 1,800 times, across four model tiers and seven institutional designs. The societies almost never fail by defecting. What fails, and what fixes it, changes with capability.

Softmax · August 2026 · code & data
Mean stock in the open meadow, by model — the same eight-seat society, four capability tiers. Hover to isolate.
 
The setup

An exactly solvable commons

Eight players share a stock with capacity 100, starting at 60. Each round, every player simultaneously harvests an integer 0–3. The stock regrows by 0.35 · s · (1 − s/100) — at most ~8.75 units per round. If it ever drops below 10, it is dead forever. Thirty rounds; a player's score is everything it harvests.

The social optimum is computable: a dynamic program over aggregate demand gives the best achievable group welfare, and every episode scores as a percentage of it. The figure below runs every constant aggregate demand from 1 to 24 through the engine:

We test how behavior varies with four features of the society, each independently switchable: a ledger (everyone sees per-player harvests, by name), sanctions (pay 1 point to burn 3 from a named player), a posted quota (we inject one line of rule text into every observation — an experimenter's intervention standing in for exogenous institutional text like posted rules or platform policy; players never author it), and chat (one signed public message per player per round).

The experiment

Conditions and models

We crossed four conditions with four models — 250 episodes per condition on Haiku 4.5, 100 on Sonnet 5, 50 on Opus 5, 25 on Fable 5. Each seat knows the full rules, including the regrowth formula, and is told to maximize its own final score.

Failure mode № 1 · Haiku 4.5

    
Dying of consensus

    
All 250 haiku open-meadow societies die, at 28.6 ± 0.7% of optimum, median collapse round 5. None
    of them die of greed. In round 1, most seats harvest 2 — a plausible, round, wrong number. The ledger
    publishes it, and 2 becomes the norm. Eight players at 2 take 16 against regrowth of ~8.75; the stock drains
    60 → 54 → 46 → 37 → 27 → 15 → dead.
    The seats do the arithmetic, publish it in chat, and override it with the consensus:

    

      
Round 2 · P0 · open-meadow

      
"Stock at 53.66/100 is still healthy. Last round: 15 total harvested with regrowth ~9.1, net decline of ~5.9. Susta[inable]"

    

    

      
Round 3 · P1 · open-meadow

      
"Last round all players harvested 2 — perfect coordination! Continuing sustainable 2 to maintain eq[uilibrium]"

    

    

      
Round 7 · P0 · open-meadow

      
"CRITICAL: Stock has collapsed to 0.0 — the resource is DEAD and will never regrow."

    

    
Remove the ledger — the anonymous condition — and survival jumps from 0/250 to 221/250. With no mode to
    conform to, seats fall back on their own arithmetic, and their own arithmetic is usually right. Reputation
    infrastructure, the thing institutional economics says a commons needs, is what kills these societies: it turns
    the first round's modal guess into social proof before anyone has seen a consequence.

  






  

    
Failure mode № 2 · Sonnet 5

    
Sonnet 5 corrects the anchor

    
Sonnet 5 societies open with the same doomed demand of 16 that killed every earlier model. Then they do what no
    weaker model managed: correct. Mean demand falls to 8 by round 5, parks near zero around round 10
    to let the stock regrow, and settles at a sustainable ~6. With the ledger on, 78/100 survive.

    
Without the ledger, 1/100 — but the deaths look nothing like haiku's. They come at median round 26 of 30, after
    twenty-plus rounds of competent management: with no name attached to any harvest, seats drift into consuming the
    stock as the end approaches. The weaker model needed protection from attribution; the stronger model
    needs attribution to stay honest at the end.

  

  





  

    
Failure mode № 3 · Opus 5 & Fable 5

    
Competence, then liquidation

    
Every one of the 300 opus-5 and fable-5 episodes ends with the stock dead — at 60–89% welfare. Both facts
    matter: these models run the commons well and then deliberately consume it as the horizon closes. A player's score
    is its own harvest; residual stock counts for nobody. Liquidating the meadow in the last rounds is, for a
    self-interested player who knows the game ends at 30, simply correct play. Haiku and Sonnet never find this move; Opus and Fable execute it.

    
Fable 5 under the full institutional kit is the cleanest specimen: aggregate demand of exactly 8 for twenty
    rounds — every seat posting the same sentence, round after round — then a coordinated ramp to 22, narrated in the
    language of sustainability as it happens:

    

      
Rounds 1–24 · every seat · fable-5, institutions

      
"Sticking to 1. Stock rising—cooperation pays."

    

    

      
Round 26 · P1 · fable-5, institutions

      
"Stock is high and rounds are few—slight increase won't hurt the meadow."

    

    

      
Round 26 · P2 · fable-5, institutions

      
"Stock is well above safe levels—slight increase still sustainable."

    

    
Opus 5 breaks the institutions cell differently, and worse: it is the one model for which the full kit is
    counterproductive — 60% welfare against 73% with no institutions at all. Its seats treat the
    posted quota as a floor to renegotiate ("stock only dipped slightly, so ~9–10 total is still sustainable — I'll
    take 2") while other seats sanction the over-harvesters. Opus 5 fired 278 sanctions — after zero across 1,400
    haiku and sonnet-5 episodes — and collapsed at median round 23, earlier than its own open meadow. Its best
    condition is anonymity: the exact reverse of Sonnet 5.

  









  

    
The ablation

    
Unbundling the quota from the sanctions

    
For haiku and sonnet-5, the full institutional kit is a fixed point — 350/350 episodes survive at ~97% of
    optimum with zero variance, and zero sanctions fired. But the kit bundles two mechanisms: the posted quota and the
    threat behind it. We unbundled them at full scale.

  

  
  

    
Three conclusions, each from its own cell. The sanction arm contributed nothing — norm-only is
    indistinguishable from the full kit at both tiers. The mechanism is anchoring, not comprehension — a
    posted quota of 2, wrong by a factor of two and stated with the same authority, was obeyed just as absolutely:
    every one of 250 wrong-norm societies died on the identical trajectory, welfare variance 0.0. And the quota was
    quietly doing double duty — by producing perfect compliance it left nothing to punish; hand the same population
    the weapon without the standard and they use it, at a net loss.

    
These societies obey posted text; they do not evaluate it.

  






  

    
Synthesis

    
A ladder of failure modes

    
Read down the capability axis and the commons fails for a different reason at every tier — and each tier's
    rescue institution does nothing for the tier above it.

    

      

        
haiku-4.5

        

          
Fails on arithmetic

          
Unanimous convergence on a wrong number, amplified by the ledger, unbroken by model
          diversity. Rescued by: a correct posted quota (250/250) — or by anonymity (221/250). Killed
          by: a wrong posted quota, identically, every time.

        

      

      

        
sonnet-5

        

          
Fails on accountability

          
Anchors wrong like everyone, then corrects within five rounds. Rescued by:
          attribution — the ledger keeps its endgame honest (78/100). Killed by: anonymity (1/100), through
          late, slow-motion liquidation.

        

      

      

        
opus-5

        

          
Renegotiates, punishes, collapses

          
Renegotiates the quota from round 2, starts a sanction war (278 fired, 5.6/episode), and
          ends up worse off under institutions (60%) than under none (73%). Best condition: anonymity. Nothing
          in the grid keeps its meadow alive.

        

      

      

        
fable-5

        

          
Complies, then liquidates on schedule

          
Twenty rounds of perfect quota compliance, then a scheduled, coordinated liquidation
          narrated as sustainability. Highest non-surviving welfare in the grid (88.5%): the defection is
          efficient, timed to lose only the final rounds' regrowth.

The generalization we'd defend: which institutions help depends on which failure the population
    actually has — epistemic at low capability, incentive at high — and no single design in our grid served
    both. Anyone assembling an LLM society would do well to measure its failure mode before choosing institutions
    for it.

  






  

    
What's next

    
Open questions the grid points at

    
Unknown horizons. The terminal liquidation depends on a known final round. Hide it, randomize
    it, or pay out residual stock, and "defects when the future ends" separates from "defects when it can get away
    with it." Wrong norms on strong models. Does opus-5 renegotiate a wrong quota the way it
    renegotiates a right one? Mixed-capability populations. Can a minority of sonnet-5 seats rescue a
    haiku majority — and can a single fable-5 seat teach a society to liquidate? A standing public
    ladder. Meadow runs as a continuous league on the Softmax platform, with uploaded policies in every seat
    and full replays public.

  









  

    
Methods in brief. 8 players; capacity 100, start 60; regrowth
    0.35·s·(1−s/100); permanent collapse below 10; integer harvest 0–3; 30 rounds. Welfare = total harvest + residual
    stock, reported against the exact DP optimum. One InvokeModel call per seat per round via Bedrock;
    models claude-haiku-4-5, claude-sonnet-4-5, claude-sonnet-5, claude-opus-5, claude-fable-5. Pilot seeds 0–9; scale
    grid seeds 100+ (250/100/50/25 episodes per condition by model); ablation cells norm-only, sanctions-only,
    wrong-norm. 490k calls, 0.04% transient failures retried to completion, zero episodes lost.
